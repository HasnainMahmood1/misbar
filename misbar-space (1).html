About
Misbar Space

Misbar Space is Saudi Arabia's dedicated space science media platform — bridging the gap between the cosmos and the curious minds of Riyadh, Jeddah, Dammam, and every city and village beyond.

Founded by a group of astronomers, journalists, and science communicators who believed that the universe deserved a Saudi voice, Misbar Space produces blogs, podcasts, news coverage, and long-form articles in both Arabic and English.

Our Mission

The Arabian Peninsula has a rich astronomical heritage — from the Bedouin navigators who read the desert sky, to the Islamic Golden Age scholars who mapped the stars, to the modern scientists who are making their mark on the global scientific community. Misbar Space exists to connect that heritage to the present, and to build a generation of Saudis who look at the night sky and see possibility rather than mystery.

We believe that science communication is not a luxury. When young people in Riyadh or Abha or Tabuk understand that the elements in their bodies were forged in dying stars — that the iron in their blood was born in a supernova — something changes in how they see themselves in relation to the universe. That change is what Misbar Space is here to make happen.

Our content spans every level of astronomical curiosity. A first-time stargazer wondering what that bright object in the western sky is will find an answer here. A student studying physics who wants to understand the mathematics behind black holes will find that too. A teacher looking for accessible, accurate resources to bring into the classroom — we have those as well.

We are particularly committed to Arabic-language science content, which remains underrepresented on the internet despite being the language of more than 400 million people. Every major piece we publish is either written in Arabic or translated into it, because we refuse to accept that Arabic-speaking audiences can only access world-class science communication through a foreign language.

The Artemis Generation: How Going Back to the Moon Will Change Everything

The Apollo programme gave humanity the overview effect — the cognitive shift that astronauts report when they see Earth hanging alone in the black void of space. Artemis will give us something more: permanence. This is not a series of expeditions. It is the beginning of a civilisation that no longer lives on a single world.

When water ice was confirmed in the permanently shadowed craters of the lunar south pole, the economics of the solar system changed overnight. Water — split by electrolysis into hydrogen and oxygen — is rocket fuel. A lunar base that can manufacture its own propellant is not merely a scientific outpost. It is a refuelling station, a stepping stone, the first node in an interplanetary infrastructure that could eventually carry humans to Mars and beyond.

Saudi Arabia's role in this future is already taking shape — the Kingdom launched its own space agency in 2018 and flew Saudi astronauts to the ISS in 2023 — but the most ambitious chapters are yet to be written. Space agencies around the world are seeking international partners for Artemis-era collaboration — in science, in engineering, in communications infrastructure. The question for Saudi institutions is not whether to engage with the new space age, but how quickly and how ambitiously to do so.

Featured Article

Are We Alone? The State of the Search for Extraterrestrial Life in 2025

The question has been asked since antiquity. In 2025, for the first time in human history, we have the instruments to begin answering it in a scientifically rigorous way — and the answers are neither confirmation nor denial, but something far more interesting: profound, accumulating ambiguity.

K2-18b may have dimethyl sulphide in its atmosphere. Europa almost certainly has a liquid ocean that has been in contact with a rocky seafloor for billions of years. Enceladus is actively venting water vapour and organic molecules into space through its south polar geysers. The Perseverance rover is collecting samples from a river delta on Mars that was once warm, wet, and rich in the chemical precursors to biology.

None of this is proof of life. All of it is evidence that the conditions for life are far more widespread in the solar system than any previous generation suspected. The universe, it appears, is not hostile to the chemistry of living things. Whether it has actually produced them — anywhere other than Earth — is the question that will define the next century of science.
